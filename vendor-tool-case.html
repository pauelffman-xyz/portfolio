Vendor Tool:
manage your restaurant yourself

I designed the Monchis vendor dashboard end to end: menu, orders, franchise view and team roles. The goal was simple: a restaurant can change a price, hide a product or add a user without calling support.

Vendors were running restaurants through phone calls and spreadsheets.

Monchis is a food delivery app in Paraguay. The restaurants that sell on it, the vendors, managed their menu and their orders through a vendor platform that had stopped keeping up.

It had two problems. Managing it took too much manual work, and it had an operating ceiling: every new branch added manual overhead instead of removing it.

What we set out to do

  • Self-managementVendors control their own menu, photos, prices, orders and team, without calling support for every change.
  • Data and AINarrow the quality gap between a professional vendor and an informal one, starting with photos and content.
  • Ecosystem growthMake the dashboard a growth channel, with touchpoints for financial services and photography tools, not only an admin panel.

What the operation told us

The evidence here is operational data, not vendor interviews. It was enough to show where the old platform broke, and each finding shaped one decision later in the case.

Two problems, seen in the operation

Fragmentation and latency (Q4)

POS was the fastest order-reception channel, at 0.9 min acceptance time, used by 41% of branches. Vendor Tool was used by the other 48%.

Service volatility (Q3)

Orders grew 15% through August, then fell 8% in September — and in that same month, Service Level dropped from 98% to 77%.

That set the brief: vendors needed stability, not just a tool.

Three decisions that shaped the tool

Coach the photo where it gets uploaded

Monchis' own product copy tells vendors that 2 out of 3 customers decide based on the photo. Photos were also the hardest thing to keep consistent across 545 independent vendors.

So the help sits where the photo is. A "Tips de fotografía" button opens a short guide: framing and the rule of thirds, natural light, and colours and textures that make a dish look good.

Every new photo then goes to the Monchis team for review before customers see it. While it waits, the product stays live with the previous photo. If it is rejected, the vendor sees why and can try again. If it is approved, it goes live with nothing more for the vendor to do.

Build the order queue around the clock

Slow acceptance was the pain, so the queue is built around time. Every pending order shows a countdown, and the card turns red as the clock runs down.

The whole order is on the card: products, add-ons, comments and payment method. A busy cashier doesn't have to open anything to decide. Accept and reject are one tap each, and selected orders move to "Preparando" together.

Give access per branch, and guard the destructive action

Franchises needed to hand daily work to other people without losing control. So access is scoped by franchise and branch, not a flat list of users. The roles table filters by franchise, branch and role, and owners create and edit users themselves, with no support ticket.

Removing a user can't be undone, so it asks for a second step: "¿Estás seguro que deseas eliminar este usuario? Recuerda que es un acción a la cual no podrás volver atrás." Are you sure you want to remove this user? This action can't be undone.

What I learned

Self-management is the test for every screen

Each job that used to need a support call became something the vendor can do alone: change a price, hide a product, add a user. I checked every screen against that.

Photo quality is a growth lever, not a cosmetic one

If 2 in 3 customers decide on the photo, coaching it at upload and reviewing it before it goes live matters more than correcting it later.

Migration is a design problem too

Moving legacy franchises onto one system was as much a UX challenge as any new feature.

If I had more time

  • Measure what the case is about. Track acceptance time and support contacts per branch, before and after.
  • Test with the people in the queue. Run a usability session on the order queue with cashiers, during a real service.
  • Hold up as the network doubles. The target is to go from 545 to 1,080 vendors (+98%), so the queue, permissions and photo review have to keep working at twice the volume.

The system behind the tool

Buttons, switches, the category picker, the queue tabs and the side navigation: built once, and reused across orders, menu and team.

Legible statuses

Built for a queue, not a dashboard demo: a status has to read at a glance during a busy service.

Forgiving destructive actions

Anything that can't be undone asks for a second step. Switching a product off can be undone with one tap.

No guessing

Every state says what happens next, so the vendor is never left wondering.
